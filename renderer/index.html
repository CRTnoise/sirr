Audio engine


            

              
              

                
                
                
              

              

                Voice — 96 kbit/s mono with echo cancellation. The default.

                Studio — 320 kbit/s stereo, all browser processing off.
                Full fidelity for music and close listening. Headphones required
                or you will echo badly.

                Low bandwidth — 24 kbit/s with silence suppression, for tethering.

                Takes effect the next time you join a channel.
              

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              

                Each audio packet carries a copy of the previous one (RFC 2198 RED),
                so a dropped packet is reconstructed instead of heard as a gap.
                Roughly doubles audio bandwidth — free on a direct peer link.
                This is the single biggest quality difference on an unstable connection.
              

            


            

              
              

                
                
                
              

              

                How much audio to hold in the playout buffer. Low (40 ms) feels
                immediate on a good link; Stable (250 ms) rides out a bad one.
                The buffer still adapts upward on its own when the network demands it.
              

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              

                Runs RNNoise locally instead of the browser's suppressor. Better on
                keyboards, fans, and street noise, and it does not duck room tone.
                Adds 10 ms of latency and a little CPU. Nothing leaves your machine.
                Falls back to your raw microphone if it cannot start.
                Takes effect the next time you join a channel.
              

            


            
Voice route


            

              
              

                
                
              

              

                Auto connects voice directly over WebRTC and, if that link
                can't be established with someone (strict NAT, blocked UDP,
                STUN unreachable), carries your voice over the same encrypted
                Hyperswarm connection your chat already uses. If you can text
                them, you can talk to them.


                Swarm only never opens a WebRTC connection, so no STUN
                server ever learns you are placing a call. Slightly higher
                latency; screen share and camera are unavailable.
                Takes effect the next time you join a channel.
              

            


            
Mesh voice — experimental


            

              
              

                
                
              

              

                Sirr's own audio pipeline instead of the browser's:
                our Opus encoder settings, our packet format, our jitter
                buffer, our redundancy. Both people need it on.


                Each direction switches to mesh only once the other side
                confirms it is actually decoding your audio, and falls back to
                standard voice within about a second if that stops — so
                it cannot leave you in a silent call.
                Experimental — expect to report bugs.
                Takes effect the next time you join a channel.
              

            


            

              
              

                
                
                
              

              

                Our own adaptive buffer, so these are real bounds rather than
                a hint to the browser. Tight targets 20–120 ms,
                Balanced 40–260 ms, Robust 80–500 ms.
                It grows quickly when the network gets worse and shrinks
                slowly when it improves. Applies live.
              

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              

                Each packet also carries a copy of an older frame, not the
                immediately previous one. That spacing is the point: it survives
                a burst of consecutive lost packets, which is the failure mode
                that actually ruins calls and the one plain RED cannot cover.
                In testing, 20% packet loss came out under 1% audible gaps.
              

            


            

              
              

                
                
                
                
              

              

                How far back the spare copy sits, in 20 ms frames. Wider
                spacing survives longer bursts; narrower recovers sooner from
                isolated losses. 3 is a good default.

What you share & who can reach you


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Incoming files are saved to Downloads as soon as they arrive.

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Let peers see the “typing…” bar while you compose.

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Fetch a title and image for links you send (never for links from others, which would reveal your IP to whoever posted them). The target site sees your IP, so it’s off by default.

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
The window shows as black in screenshots, recordings and screen shares on Windows and macOS — including malware that takes screenshots. It can’t stop a camera pointed at the screen.

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Listen on a public inbox so people you share no room with can send you a message request. Anyone who has seen your fingerprint can then find your IP address while you are online, so it’s off by default.

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
When someone in the room is offline, a sealed copy of your text messages waits inside the DHT itself — no server, no member holding it — and is found when they come back, even if you’re gone by then. It lives 48 h and every member who reads it keeps it alive another 48 h. Trade-off: echoes are sealed with a key from the room code, not a forward-secret session — someone recording DHT traffic who later gets the room code could read that window.

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Every link also carries encrypted chaff at random moments, padded like real messages, so someone watching your connection can’t tell when you are talking. Costs a little bandwidth (about 1 KB per peer every few seconds).

            


            

              
              

                
                
                
              

              
Hold each message for a random moment before it leaves (order is kept). Blurs typing rhythm and how fast who answers whom. All messages are already padded to fixed sizes.

            


            
Getting through — censorship & shutdowns

            

              
              
—

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Find room members on the same Wi-Fi or hotspot (and over Bluetooth where available) with no internet at all. Every nearby device running Sirr relays encrypted beacons a few hops further, so the more people use it the further it reaches. Relays never see contents or rooms. Someone on the same network can tell a Sirr device is present.

            


            

              
              
              
The DHT is normally entered through three public nodes; if those are blocked, Sirr tries nodes it remembers from earlier sessions, then these. Invite links carry a couple of entry points too.

            


            

              
              
              

              
Carry all of Sirr through one WebSocket to a bridge someone runs for you. Behind HTTPS it looks like an ordinary website, so traffic filters that recognise peer-to-peer UDP don’t see Sirr. The bridge sees only end-to-end ciphertext, but it can see your IP and when you’re online.

            


            

              
              

                
                
                
              

              
When two people can’t reach each other directly (mobile networks, strict NAT), a volunteer’s blind relay passes the encrypted stream along. It can’t read it.

            


            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Relay encrypted streams for up to 24 strangers who can’t connect directly. Costs your bandwidth while Sirr runs; new relay key every launch.

            


            

              
              
              
Only useful on a machine others can reach (public IP or a forwarded port). Put it behind a TLS proxy on :443 so it looks like a website, then give the wss:// address to people who need it.

            


            

              
              

                
                
                
              

              

            


            

              
              

            

          


          
          

            
Backup & restore


            

              
              
              

                
                
              

              
Bundles your identity key, rooms, contacts, pins and message history into one file sealed with the passphrase above (scrypt + XSalsa20-Poly1305). Choose a long passphrase: the file can be attacked offline. Importing replaces your current data and requires a restart.

App lock

            

              
              
              
              
              

                
                
                
              

              

              
Seals your data key and identity under the passphrase as well as the OS keychain: a laptop taken while you’re logged in still opens to a lock screen. Typing the duress passphrase there silently destroys everything and Sirr opens as a fresh install. There is no recovery if you forget the passphrase.

            

            

              
              

                
                
              

            

            

              
              

                
                
                
                
              

              
Locking restarts Sirr into the lock screen, so no key stays in memory.

            


            
Panic

            

              
              
              

              
Shreds the data key and identity key first — from that instant every message, file and room code on disk is unreadable — then deletes the rest and quits. Other members keep what they received.

            

            

              
              

                
                
              

              
Works system-wide, even with Sirr in the tray. No confirmation.
